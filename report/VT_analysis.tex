\subsection*{Key takeaways}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.2em]
  \item Over 18.3 years, VT returned 8.8\% a year with dividends reinvested, of which roughly 2.3\% a year came from dividends.
  \item The price of that return was 20\% annual volatility and a maximum drawdown of 50\% during the global financial crisis.
  \item Daily returns are clearly non-normal (kurtosis of 16.1) and volatility clusters over time; their direction is only weakly predictable from one day to the next, and not at all over longer horizons.
  \item For a Swiss franc investor, the weaker dollar cut the annualised return by 1.3\% a year (7.5\% in CHF).
\end{itemize}

Apart from a small effect at the first lag (see Section~3), returns show little autocorrelation (Ljung--Box with 10 lags: $Q = 89.9$). Squared returns, by contrast, are strongly and persistently autocorrelated ($Q = 4,305$, $p < 0.001$). A large move tends to be followed by further large moves of either sign, the behaviour captured by ARCH and GARCH models.

\note{$t$-statistic approximated by $\hat\rho_1\sqrt{n}$ (standard error $1/\sqrt{n}$ under the random-walk hypothesis). It overstates significance when volatility clusters; the robust version of this test is the variance ratio $VR(2)$ below.}

Daily autocorrelation is negative ($-$0.105): a gain tends to be slightly reversed the following day. The effect is concentrated in crises: excluding 2008--2009 and February to June 2020, it falls to $-$0.044, still negative but less than half as large. Possible explanations include overreaction on turbulent days, or temporary gaps between the ETF price and the value of its foreign holdings, which trade at different hours. At weekly and monthly frequencies, no significant autocorrelation appears.

\keep{22}\subsection{Variance ratio test}
Under the random-walk hypothesis, the variance of $q$-period returns is $q$ times the one-period variance, so
\[
VR(q) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(r_t + r_{t-1} + \dots + r_{t-q+1}\right)}{q \,\operatorname{Var}(r_t)} = 1 .
\]
A ratio below 1 signals mean reversion, a ratio above 1 persistence. Following \citet{lo1988}, the test statistic $z^*(q)$ is robust to heteroskedasticity, which is essential given the volatility clustering documented above.

\begin{table}[H]
\small
\caption{Lo--MacKinlay variance ratio test on daily log total returns.}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{l R R R R}
\toprule
\textbf{Horizon $q$ (days)} & \textbf{$VR(q)$} & \textbf{$z^*(q)$} & \textbf{$p$-value} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
2 & 0.895 & $-$2.97 & 0.003 & mean reversion \\
5 & 0.830 & $-$2.08 & 0.037 & mean reversion \\
10 & 0.778 & $-$1.80 & 0.072 & not rejected \\
20 & 0.760 & $-$1.38 & 0.167 & not rejected \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}
The random-walk hypothesis is rejected at the 5\% level for $q = 2, 5$. Even where a deviation is statistically significant, it is small in magnitude and would need to survive transaction costs to be exploitable.

\keep{22}\subsection{The cost of missing the best days}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{best_days.pdf}
\caption{Final value of USD 10{,}000 invested at inception, with and without the best trading days.}
\end{figure}
Missing the 10 best days out of 4,614 cuts the annualised return from 8.8\% to 4.1\%. The reverse exercise is just as striking: avoiding the 10 worst days would have lifted the final value to USD 114,446. 7 of the 10 best days fall within two weeks of one of the 10 worst. They occur in the same crisis periods: avoiding the worst days without missing the best would require perfect timing.

\keep{22}\subsection{Monthly savings plan and lump-sum investing}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{savings.pdf}
\caption{Left: value of a USD 500 monthly plan vs amount paid in. Right: lump sum minus 12-month averaging, value gap after 12 months, by starting month.}
\end{figure}
Investing USD 500 at the end of every month since inception (220 contributions, USD 110,000 in total) would be worth USD 344,497 today, an internal rate of return of 11.3\% a year.

Across the 209 possible monthly starting points, investing a sum all at once beat spreading it over 12 months (with the uninvested cash earning the T-bill rate) in 72\% of cases, with an average advantage of 4.5\% after 12 months. Because the market rises most of the time, waiting costs money on average; averaging in mainly limits the regret of investing just before a decline.
